FUE, explained: Advanced, Core and Self-Service in plain English

If you have looked at an S/4HANA quote and wondered why one named user costs five times another, start here. The unit SAP bills in is simple; what makes it expensive is how a user ends up in a tier.

Aug 18, 20266 min readBy the LicenseIQ team

One unit, three kinds of user

SAP prices S/4HANA named users in Full Use Equivalents, or FUE. The unit exists so that very different kinds of user can be pooled into one number on a contract. The conversion is fixed:

Use typeUsers per FUEFUE per userTypical work
Advanced Use11.00Creates and changes business objects: orders, invoices, master data
Core Use50.20Transacts within a defined scope: approvals, confirmations, requisitions
Self-Service Use300.03Looks things up, records time, raises requests

A contract buys a block of FUE. You fill it with whatever mix of users you have, and the mix is where the money moves. (SAP also defines a Developer Use type, which most estates manage separately and we leave aside here.)

What decides a user's tier

Not what a user does. What they could do. The tier a user needs follows from the authorizations in their roles. SAP classifies transactions and apps by use type, so a role that contains even one Advanced-classified authorization is an Advanced role, and a user who holds one Advanced role is an Advanced user, whatever else they hold and however rarely they use it.

That mechanism is behind most over-licensing, and it has a name: inheritance. A job profile built years ago bundles a dozen roles. One of them carries a maintain-transaction nobody in that job has run since the project went live. Every person in the profile is an Advanced user at 1 FUE, while their actual work is Core at 0.2.

The arithmetic of a single role

Remove one role from a job profile and every user in it drops from 1 FUE to 0.2. At $540 per FUE that is $432 a year, per person, for as long as the contract runs.

Take a profile with 150 users. Today it costs 150 FUE. Reclassified to Core it costs 30 FUE, freeing 120. At a reference price of $540 per FUE that is $64,800 a year; at a contract price of $2,000 per FUE it is $240,000. The role itself is unchanged for the people who still need it; it has simply stopped being inherited by the people who don't.

Why usage is the evidence

Authorizations tell you the tier a user is being charged for. Usage statistics tell you the tier they actually need. Compare the two for every user, the highest tier they exercised against the highest tier they hold, and you get three things at once: the candidates for a change, the exact roles that cause the difference, and the saving in FUE and in dollars.

Reference assessment. Of 7,149 active users, 598 (8.4%) held a higher tier than their usage required. Moving them freed 308 FUE, a 7% reduction in the estate's FUE count, before counting a single idle license.

This comparison is the core of LicenseIQ's license optimization: current FUE from roles, proposed FUE from observed transactions, and the difference priced at your rate. Every row names the user, the roles and the usage that produced it.

Three things to take into your next conversation

  1. Ask for the role classification file. The mapping of roles to use types is the key input. If nobody can produce it, your FUE count is being set by a rule you cannot see.
  2. Hunt for the single Advanced role in otherwise-Core job profiles. It is the most common pattern and the cheapest to fix.
  3. Price everything in FUE first, then in dollars at your contract rate. It keeps the conversation with procurement in the same unit the vendor uses.
